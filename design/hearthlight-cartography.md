# Hearthlight Cartography

A movement for rooms seen from a little above, the way a traveller remembers a place after the second visit: not the whole of it, but where the light pooled, where the floor was worn, which shelf leaned. The camera hangs at a three-quarter height. Floors spread out like a map; everything that stands up turns its front face toward the viewer and lets its top catch the sky. Depth is never faked with perspective lines. It is earned through stacking: a lip of shadow under every ledge, a darker band down every front face, a lighter rim along every top edge.

Every object is built from the pixel up as if by a craftsman who has carved the same tavern stool a thousand times and still finds one more knot in the grain. Outlines are dark and chunky but never black; they take the hue of the shadow beneath them, so the whole scene sits inside one atmosphere. Each surface carries three to four values only, chosen with painstaking restraint, so that the eye reads material before it reads colour: oiled wood, river stone, moss-soft velvet, iron gone blue at the edges. The work must look like the product of countless patient hours by someone at the very top of the pixel-art discipline, meticulously crafted down to the last stray highlight.

Colour is a climate, not a decoration. One warm source and one cool ambient govern every room. The warm source spills from hearths, lanterns and enchanted glass; the ambient settles into corners and under furniture. Saturation is spent only where light lands. Everything else is quiet, slightly desaturated, so that the few glowing things feel precious. A master-level palette of perhaps twenty-four colours per room, each one placed with deliberate intent, never a gradient where a hand-placed dither would do.

Space is generous and legible. Floors stay open in broad lanes so small figures can walk and be seen; furniture gathers into clusters with their own gravity, each cluster a small stage. Walls frame the top of the composition like the spine of a book; the lower edge stays open, inviting entry. Rhythm comes from repetition with variation: planks that almost repeat, books that lean differently, flagstones whose cracks never align twice. Painstaking attention to these near-repetitions is what makes a room feel lived in rather than generated.

Text barely exists. If any mark appears it is carved, stitched or painted into the world itself: a rune on a banner, a notice pinned to a board, illegible at a glance and meant to be. The image is the information. It should reward a second look and a tenth, each pass revealing another meticulously placed detail, the unmistakable signature of deep expertise and an obsessive, loving hand.
